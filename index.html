I

Part one

The BBC Proms Festival

The BBC Proms (official name: The Henry Wood Promenade Concerts) is an eight-week summer festival of classical music held annually in the United Kingdom.

2026 season dates. The festival takes place every year from mid-July to mid-September. For the 2026 edition, it runs from 17 July to 12 September 2026.

Principal venue

The vast majority of the more than 70 concerts are held at the Royal Albert Hall in London, alongside regional events at alternative venues like Cadogan Hall or around the country.

Origins

Founded in 1895 by impresario Robert Newman and conductor Sir Henry Wood, the festival aimed to make classical music accessible to a wider, general audience.

The “Promming” spirit

The term “prom” derives from “promenade concert”, historically referring to concerts where audiences could walk around or stand. Today, highly affordable standing tickets (“Proms Passes” or tickets purchased on the day) allow the audience to experience the music from right up close to the stage in the central Arena or up in the Gallery.

Programmatic diversity

The festival combines major classical orchestral works with jazz, world music, contemporary classical premieres and newly commissioned works. All concerts are broadcast live and relayed by the BBC, particularly on BBC Radio 3.

Part two

The History of the Last Night of the Proms

The Last Night of the Proms is far more than a simple closing concert; it is a profound British cultural institution renowned worldwide for its eccentric, celebratory and patriotic atmosphere. This unique ritual was shaped by specific historical milestones.

  1. 1895–1939Festive beginnings

    Festive end-of-season beginnings

    From the festival's birth in 1895 under Robert Newman and Sir Henry Wood, the closing concert intentionally adopted a lighter mood than the rest of the season. In 1905, Sir Henry Wood composed the Fantasia on British Sea Songs, a suite of traditional maritime melodies, to mark the centenary of the Battle of Trafalgar. The piece was an instant hit, becoming an annual tradition and forming the bedrock of the Last Night's musical customs.

  2. 1941The wartime turning point

    The Second World War turning point

    The patriotic tone and structural format we know today were forged during the Blitz. In 1941, the festival's original venue, Queen's Hall, was completely destroyed by German bombing raids, prompting an emergency relocation to the Royal Albert Hall. That same year, Sir Henry Wood established the tradition of the conductor's closing speech to thank the audience and advocate for live music. Against the wartime backdrop, the patriotic hymns took on a profound collective emotional weight.

  3. 1954The running order fixed

    The codification of traditional anthems

    The permanent structure of the concert's second half was explicitly codified in 1954, dividing the final night into two distinct portions.

    First half

    Remains dedicated to standard serious classical repertoire, frequently incorporating modern premieres or internationally acclaimed soloists.

    Second half

    The celebratory, interactive singalong, in which the audience joins in unison for a fixed sequence of patriotic favourites.

  4. FolkloreThe Promenaders

    The folklore of the Promenaders

    Over the decades, the standing audience in the central Arena (known as the “Promenaders”) developed unique, humorous subcultural rituals.

    Fancy dress & flags. Spectators arrive in extravagant costumes and eccentric hats, waving thousands of Union Jacks alongside flags from dozens of nations representing international music lovers.

    The Hornpipe choreography. During the “Jack's the Lad” (Sailor's Hornpipe) segment of Henry Wood's suite, the standing audience jumps up and down in synchronised rhythm, accelerating at a frenetic pace as the orchestra builds to its rapid climax.

  5. 1996–2013Reach & renewal

    Global reach and modern developments

    Today, the Last Night is televised live across the globe by the BBC to millions of viewers. Due to overwhelming demand for tickets, the BBC launched the Proms in the Park events in 1996, creating parallel big-screen outdoor concerts across major UK cities (such as London, Belfast, Glasgow and Cardiff) to link with the Royal Albert Hall.

    The festival has also modernised its line-up; in 2013, American conductor Marin Alsop broke historical ground by becoming the first ever woman to conduct the Last Night of the Proms.

Part three

Constitutional Status of UK National Anthems

Strictly speaking, within the British constitutional framework, the only official national anthem for the entirety of the United Kingdom is God Save the King. The four constituent nations (England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland) do not possess separate home anthems universally enacted by individual national acts of parliament. Instead, sport and cultural tradition have established distinct de facto, unofficial or semi-official regional anthems.

The United Kingdom

Official, by custom God Save the King

God Save the King (or Queen, depending on the reigning monarch) serves as the official national anthem, rooted in historical royal custom rather than codified statutory law. Rule, Britannia! and Land of Hope and Glory function as secondary, highly prominent unofficial patriotic anthems.

The Sex Pistols' “God Save the Queen” (1977)

Released in May 1977 to coincide with Elizabeth II's Silver Jubilee, the Sex Pistols' single borrowed the anthem's title to turn it against the monarchy and the national mood of celebration. Banned by the BBC and refused by most shops, it nonetheless climbed to number two in Jubilee week, behind Rod Stewart, which fuelled lasting suspicions that the chart had been quietly adjusted; the band's boat trip down the Thames on 7 June, playing the song outside Parliament, ended in arrests. The record remains the defining punk counter-anthem: a parody that confirmed, by the outrage it provoked, how sacred the original still was.

England

No legal anthem God Save the King · Jerusalem

England possesses no unique legal national anthem of its own. God Save the King is used by default at international football and rugby fixtures.

However, Jerusalem (William Blake's poem set to music by Hubert Parry) is the officially designated anthem for England at the Commonwealth Games and is played at international cricket matches. Land of Hope and Glory was previously used for the Commonwealth Games before being succeeded by Jerusalem.

Scotland

De facto Flower of Scotland

Scotland possesses no legally codified national anthem. Flower of Scotland (composed in 1967 by the folk group The Corries) has become the de facto national anthem, universally adopted by the Scottish rugby union team, the national football team and the Commonwealth Games delegation since the 1990s/2010s. Traditional historical alternatives include Scotland the Brave (heavily associated with bagpipes) and Scots Wha Hae.

Wales

Semi-official Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau

Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau (Land of My Fathers) enjoys stable, highly institutionalised semi-official de facto status. While lacking formal statutory legislation, it is officially recognised by the Welsh Government and the Senedd, performed at major state events before the Royal Family, and sung entirely in the Welsh language at sporting fixtures.

Northern Ireland

No consensus Depends on the sport

Owing to deeply rooted political and communal divisions, Northern Ireland has no single national anthem that commands cross-community consensus. Usage depends strictly on the governing sporting body: God Save the King is used at official UK state functions and by the Northern Ireland national football team.

The Londonderry Air (popularly sung to the lyrics of Danny Boy) serves as the politically neutral official anthem for the Northern Irish delegation at the Commonwealth Games. Ireland's Call, written in 1995 to serve as a unifying anthem, is used by sporting bodies that organise on an all-island basis, uniting the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland under a single banner (such as rugby union, hockey and cricket).

Sporting Anthems and Stadium Chants

Alongside regional national anthems, the United Kingdom boasts an immensely rich culture of crowd-led terrace chants and popular sporting anthems that echo across football and rugby stadiums, serving as powerful markers of sporting pride.

England Rugby & football

Rugby union · Twickenham

Swing Low, Sweet Chariot

The quintessential anthem of the English rugby team at Twickenham. Originally a 19th-century African-American spiritual, it was spontaneously adopted by spectators in 1988 to support the Black English winger Chris Oti during a hat-trick performance. It remains a massive fan favourite.

Football · Euro 96

Three Lions (It's Coming Home)

Composed for Euro 96 by comedians David Baddiel and Frank Skinner alongside the alternative rock band The Lightning Seeds, it stands as the ultimate anthem of English football fans. The iconic line “It's coming home” frames the historic yearning and self-deprecating optimism of England fans waiting for a major trophy since 1966.

Rugby · Unofficial

Delilah

Tom Jones's iconic 1968 pop hit became the legendary unofficial anthem for Welsh rugby fans inside Cardiff's Principality Stadium. Although the Welsh Rugby Union banned the song from stadium loudspeakers and official choirs in 2023 because of the domestic-violence themes in its lyrics, fans continue to sing it passionately a cappella from the stands.
